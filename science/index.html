01 Deep space

Seven worlds around a small red star

TRAPPIST-1 is a cool red dwarf about 40 light-years away with seven Earth-sized planets, all found by the way they dim the star as they pass in front of it. Their years run from 1.5 to 19 days, locked in a chain of near-whole-number ratios, and three of them orbit where liquid water could last.

Drag across the system to run time faster or slower. The trace below is the starlight dimming at each transit.

02 The geometry of plantae

Why a sunflower turns by 137.5°

Many plants set each new seed or leaf about 137.5° around from the last: the golden angle. Packed that way, seeds never line up into gaps, and the head shows interlocking spirals whose counts are neighbouring Fibonacci numbers, such as 34 and 55. Nudge the angle by a fraction of a degree and the pattern falls apart.

Move the slider away from the golden angle and watch the spirals break.

03 The extreme beauty of animalia

Colour with no pigment, flocks with no leader

A morpho butterfly’s blue is not a dye. Nanoscale ridges on its wing scales reflect light back in layers that interfere, so the colour shifts as you tilt your view. A starling murmuration has no leader either: each bird keeps track of its six or seven nearest neighbours, and thousands of those small decisions make one shape.

Move across the wing to tilt it. Move into the flock to scatter it.

04 Deep time

The mountains that made life

Twice in Earth’s history, as supercontinents came together, mountain ranges as high as the Himalayas and up to 8,000 km long rose across them. They are known only from their roots: tiny zircon crystals, low in lutetium, that form under the pressure deep beneath high mountains. As those supermountains wore away they washed phosphorus and iron into the oceans. The first, the Nuna Supermountain, 2.0 to 1.8 billion years ago, coincides with the likely appearance of eukaryotes, the complex cells that plants and animals are made of. The second, the Transgondwanan, 650 to 500 million years ago, coincides with the first large animals and, about 45 million years later, the Cambrian explosion. In between, with no supermountains, came the “Boring Billion”, when life barely changed. It is a correlation the researchers propose to test, not a proven cause.

Move across the timeline to stop it at any moment; the gold and blue grains are the nutrients reaching the sea.

05 Human discovery and mystery

Still listening

Voyager 1 left Earth in 1977, crossed into interstellar space in 2012, and is the farthest thing people have ever made, still sending word home across a gap light takes most of a day to cross. The Drake equation, written in 1961, is how we argue about who else might be out there to hear: seven factors, most of them still guesses.

06 Computer science

A model cast in silicon

A GPU runs a language model as software: its billions of weights stream in from memory for every word it writes, and memory, not arithmetic, sets the pace. Taalas turned that around. Its first chip, the HC1, builds one model, Llama 3.1 8B, into the silicon itself, its weights stored on the die beside the circuits that use them, in a 6 nm chip of 53 billion transistors. The company reports about 17,000 tokens a second for a single user, against about 230 on an Nvidia H200 (independent tests measured 14,000 to 16,000), and says a new model can be cast into a chip in about two months. The cost of all that speed: the HC1 can run that one model and nothing else.

Both lanes start together, in real time. The chip has finished before the GPU has written its first sentence.

07 Computer science and physics

The algorithms behind this site

Everything that moves on these pages is computed as you watch, with no video and no renders. These are the algorithms that do it, by field, and where you can see each one. Every band on the homepage lists its own under the i in its corner.

Computer science 9

  • Depth-first maze carving

    A random spanning tree of the screen’s cells: the carver steps to an unvisited neighbour until it is boxed in, then backs up, which makes long winding corridors.

    Clicking into About Me, Résumé or Contact

  • Stateless instanced particles

    Thousands of cubes, streamlines, sheets, film strips, sparks and ink strokes, one draw call per kind; each position is a pure function of the scroll, so scrolling back un-builds the scene exactly.

    The homepage’s transitions

  • Greedy list scheduling

    Where and when each of the vortex’s cubes lands: landing slots come in the order the cubes leave, and each goes, through a priority queue, to the cell first in the building order (low and outward first) of those whose paper has already gone. So the bottom corners are built first, then upward and inward, and no cube lands on paper. It is planned once per screen size and handed to the shaders as a small texture.

    Arriving at Science

  • Precomputed fields

    The ice’s geometry and padded arrival atlases are prepared in a worker. The vortex schedule, ink reach and click maze are also prepared once and read from textures.

    The transitions

  • Dynamic resolution

    A feedback loop on frame time: the renderer steps its detail, then its resolution, down when frames run long (a run of slow frames, or too large a share of them missing their frame over the last second) and back up when they are quick, and hands the pages back to their CSS art if even half resolution is too slow.

    Every page

  • Latest-intent navigation

    Every click or history step is an intent with its own abortable fetch; a newer one cancels the older, so the page you land on is always the last one asked for.

    Every page

  • Painter’s algorithm

    The 48 peaks are drawn tallest first, so nearer, lower ones overlap them as in a real range.

    The 48 skyline, on the homepage and Outdoors

  • Legal-move generation and SAN parsing

    Games are read move by move from standard algebraic notation into positions, each move checked against the rules (chess.js).

Physics 20

  • The Burgers vortex

    An exact solution of the Navier–Stokes equations: fluid drawn in toward an axis spins faster as it closes in, keeping its angular momentum, and is stretched along the axis. Scrolled, the vortex stands on the foot of the screen and grows taller; the page is gathered into cubes up the screen behind its top, each height over a long window, so some cubes lag behind. Each cube goes round the vortex one and a half to four and a half times, rising with the wind, and is thrown down and out onto the page it builds. Clicked, the cubes come out of the axis at the link and spiral away to build the page. Every cell sends one cube and receives one.

    Arriving at Science, scrolled or clicked

  • Density waves

    The ring’s cubes bunch into five swarms, as stars bunch into a galaxy’s spiral arms: each cube’s angle is drawn toward the nearest crest of a wave that turns with the wind, so the swarms keep turning, even while the page is still, as cubes pass through them.

    Arriving at Science

  • Divergence-free vortex field

    Three layers of drifting vortex cells, the curl of a stream function, so whatever follows it swirls without bunching up.

    The ink before Chess and ice dust before Outdoors

  • Coloured dew

    Between the cores a fine mist condenses where the nebula is thick, once its gas has cooled, each site after its own random delay drawn from classical nucleation theory, J ∝ exp(−B / ln² S) (on a click, behind a cold front from the click), and grows by a constant condensation flux, as the cube root of time. Every drop holds its gas’s colour. Drops that touch merge, keeping their volume (r³ = r₁³ + r₂³) at the volume-weighted centroid and mixing their colours by volume, a merge snapping shut with a damped wobble (Rayleigh’s l = 2 mode) and, its contact line pinned, never quite relaxing to a circle.

    Arriving at Racing, scrolled or clicked

  • Every drop a lens

    Each drop is a spherical cap, traced exactly: Snell’s law at the curved surface, then out through the flat pane. A drop looks off its axis toward the far side, so what it shows (the nebula behind it) is inverted and minified, with the focal length of a plano-convex lens, R/(n − 1), each colour bent its own amount. Its colour follows Beer–Lambert’s law through the depth of the cap, T = c^(h/ℓ), so a small drop is pale and a large one deep, its middle deepest. Fresnel reflection (Schlick) shows a dark room and a four-paned window; the stardust it gathered still turns inside it for a while. Artistic: water’s dispersion drawn three times over; on a click, the pane looks onto Racing through a window that widens as the dew ages.

    Arriving at Racing, scrolled or clicked

  • Supernovae and planetary nebulae

    Science’s sky has a dozen supergiants, each coloured as a black body: Planck’s law integrated against the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions, red at 3,400 K, blue-white at 30,000 K. Moving on to Racing, a few of them end, chosen so that each nebula has room of its own and only the outer fringes of neighbours cross. The hot ones go supernova: each pulses faster and faster, dims as its core collapses, then breaks out in a flash fading along the light curve of nickel-56 decaying to cobalt-56 (mean lives 8.8 and 111.3 days, compressed but in proportion); a light echo races out through the dust, and the gas near it flares in [O III], then glows in Hα as it recombines; the remnant expands freely (R ∝ t), then as a Sedov–Taylor blast wave (R ∝ t^⅖). Each is a different remnant: the Crab (a pulsar flickering in blue-white synchrotron light inside a cage of red and orange filaments), Cassiopeia A (a lop-sided ring of fast knots, oxygen-green on one side and sulphur-red on the other, with a jet), the Veil (braided arcs, each strand red with hydrogen inside and blue-green with oxygen outside, the layering of a cooling shock) and SN 1987A (a tilted ring of hot spots lighting one by one as the ejecta reach it, two fainter rings above and below). The cool giants shed planetary nebulae instead, their cores left as white dwarfs: the Cat’s Eye (nested, tilted bubbles, jets and a halo of rings), the Ring (a torus layered blue-green to red, dark knots on its inner edge) and the Butterfly (two lobes either side of a dusty waist). Artistic: the pace, compressed from millennia to a scroll.

    Science’s sky, and arriving at Racing by scroll

  • Gravitational collapse: stardust spiralling in

    Behind each shock the gas breaks into cores, most where its nebula is densest, each the start of a drop. A core falls in on itself as a cloud in free fall: a grain that starts at r₀ is at r = r₀ cos²β when t / t_ff = (β + sin β cos β) / (π/2), at rest at first and ever faster. It keeps its angular momentum, so r² dθ/dt stays constant and θ − θ₀ = Ω tan β: an exact spiral that quickens as it closes. Each grain is drawn as a glint with a trail along its orbit, so every drop gathers a whirlpool of its gas’s colours. The outer shells arrive later, at a steady rate (Shu’s inside-out collapse), so each drop’s volume grows linearly as its grains arrive; the gas streams in too, squeezed brighter as it gathers, and the nebula fades as its gas is taken. The drops’ sizes follow a power law, n(r) ∝ r^−2, a few large and many small. Artistic: liquid water from a nebula’s gas.

    Arriving at Racing, scrolled

  • A freezing chain reaction

    A few seeds of ice at the frame’s edges set it off. Each frozen drop grows a bridge of frost across the gap to its neighbours at a steady rate, and a drop freezes when the first bridge reaches it: first-passage percolation over the drops (interdroplet ice bridging, Boreyko & Collier, 2013), so the freeze runs across the pane as a branching tree, flash after flash, the middle last; where the drops are too far apart to bridge, the frost front reaches them across the bare glass. Each drop freezes in two stages: dendritic ice fills it milky in a flash (recalescence), then it clears to a bead of ice in its own colour. Its latent heat evaporates the drops nearby, and the vapour freezes out in a ring, a frost halo (Jung, Tiwari & Poulikakos, 2012). On a click the freeze runs out from the click instead.

    Arriving at Racing, scrolled or clicked

  • The rift and the field lines

    The main crack opens with the elliptical crack-opening profile of linear elastic fracture (Griffith, Irwin): widest mid-span, closed at its ends. A fracture front runs out from it at a steady speed, and each piece breaks away once the front has run round it, coming apart into fragments. The crack is charged and Racing’s card oppositely, a little more strongly, so every electric field line ends on the card (a two-dimensional Coulomb field, the lines traced with Runge–Kutta). Each fragment, overdamped, moves along the field line through where it was, slowly at first and quicker as the field strengthens, so the pieces are drawn in, vacuumed into the card; each turns to lie along the field (the torque on its induced dipole), spins about it so its face flashes, and fuses into the card where its line ends. The field itself glows as it builds, every line lit behind its fragment, the pattern of a magnet’s filings on a screen. The card frosts from where they land, snow crystals of every habit nucleating and growing into one another, flashes and anneals clear into Racing’s glass card. Artistic: lightning bridging the gap until it snaps, a sunrise behind the ice with god rays and ice dust, the page’s text condensing from frost.

Mathematics 14

  • Snow crystals, fissures and the main crack

    As the freeze reaches them, the larger drops (kept a minimum distance apart) seed snow crystals in their own colours, each of its own habit from the Nakaya diagram, mostly stellar and fernlike dendrites, with sectored, broad-branched and stellar plates, the conditions varying smoothly over the pane. Side branches leave each arm at 60° at irregular places and of irregular lengths, nearly but never exactly the same on all six arms, and the longer ones branch again, so no two crystals are alike. Hoarfrost feathers in behind them, frozen drops dim under their plates, and grains of frost glitter. Where two crystals meet the boundary splits, about two in three of them, into a fissure. The main crack is the least-cost path through the Voronoi graph from the left edge to the right, kept near the middle (Dijkstra); the pane comes apart along it (see The rift and the field lines). On a click the cells fall instead, each shrinking into the distance about its centroid, from the click outward. Artistic: the colours the ice takes from the nebulae, the electricity crawling and flickering along the fissures and surging along the main crack, and the shaking.
