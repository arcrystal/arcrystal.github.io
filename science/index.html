01 Deep space

Seven worlds around a small red star

TRAPPIST-1 is a cool red dwarf about 40 light-years away with seven Earth-sized planets, all found by the way they dim the star as they pass in front of it. Their years run from 1.5 to 19 days, locked in a chain of near-whole-number ratios, and three of them orbit where liquid water could last.

Drag across the system to run time faster or slower. The trace below is the starlight dimming at each transit.

02 The geometry of plantae

Why a sunflower turns by 137.5°

Many plants set each new seed or leaf about 137.5° around from the last: the golden angle. Packed that way, seeds never line up into gaps, and the head shows interlocking spirals whose counts are neighbouring Fibonacci numbers, such as 34 and 55. Nudge the angle by a fraction of a degree and the pattern falls apart.

Move the slider away from the golden angle and watch the spirals break.

03 The extreme beauty of animalia

Colour with no pigment, flocks with no leader

A morpho butterfly’s blue is not a dye. Nanoscale ridges on its wing scales reflect light back in layers that interfere, so the colour shifts as you tilt your view. A starling murmuration has no leader either: each bird keeps track of its six or seven nearest neighbours, and thousands of those small decisions make one shape.

Move across the wing to tilt it. Move into the flock to scatter it.

04 Deep time

The mountains that made life

Twice in Earth’s history, as supercontinents came together, mountain ranges as high as the Himalayas and up to 8,000 km long rose across them. They are known only from their roots: tiny zircon crystals, low in lutetium, that form under the pressure deep beneath high mountains. As those supermountains wore away they washed phosphorus and iron into the oceans. The first, the Nuna Supermountain, 2.0 to 1.8 billion years ago, coincides with the likely appearance of eukaryotes, the complex cells that plants and animals are made of. The second, the Transgondwanan, 650 to 500 million years ago, coincides with the first large animals and, about 45 million years later, the Cambrian explosion. In between, with no supermountains, came the “Boring Billion”, when life barely changed. It is a correlation the researchers propose to test, not a proven cause.

Move across the timeline to stop it at any moment; the gold and blue grains are the nutrients reaching the sea.

05 Human discovery and mystery

Still listening

Voyager 1 left Earth in 1977, crossed into interstellar space in 2012, and is the farthest thing people have ever made, still sending word home across a gap light takes most of a day to cross. The Drake equation, written in 1961, is how we argue about who else might be out there to hear: seven factors, most of them still guesses.

06 Computer science

A model cast in silicon

A GPU runs a language model as software: its billions of weights stream in from memory for every word it writes, and memory, not arithmetic, sets the pace. Taalas turned that around. Its first chip, the HC1, builds one model, Llama 3.1 8B, into the silicon itself, its weights stored on the die beside the circuits that use them, in a 6 nm chip of 53 billion transistors. The company reports about 17,000 tokens a second for a single user, against about 230 on an Nvidia H200 (independent tests measured 14,000 to 16,000), and says a new model can be cast into a chip in about two months. The cost of all that speed: the HC1 can run that one model and nothing else.

Both lanes start together, in real time. The chip has finished before the GPU has written its first sentence.

07 Computer science and physics

The algorithms behind this site

Everything that moves on these pages is computed as you watch, with no video and no renders. These are the algorithms that do it, by field, and where you can see each one. Every band on the homepage lists its own under the i in its corner.

Computer science 14

  • Depth-first maze carving

    A random spanning tree of the screen’s cells: the carver steps to an unvisited neighbour until it is boxed in, then backs up, which makes long winding corridors.

    Clicking into About Me, Résumé or Contact

  • Stateless instanced particles

    Thousands of cubes, streamlines, drops, shards and embers, one draw call per kind; each position is a pure function of the scroll, so scrolling back un-builds the scene exactly.

  • Greedy list scheduling

    Where and when each of the vortex’s cubes lands: landing slots come in the order the cubes leave, and each goes, through a priority queue, to the cell first in the building order (low and outward first) of those whose paper has already gone. So the bottom corners are built first, then upward and inward, and no cube lands on paper. It is planned once per screen size and handed to the shaders as a small texture.

  • Paint finding its own land: competing fronts

    The blotches look random, but each is the colour of the land where it fell (the hardwoods’ yellows, oranges and reds; the spruce and fir’s green; white high up), and soon its paint creeps out over a 256 × 256 grid of the map by a multi-source Dijkstra: each cell taken by whichever front reaches it first, a front moving fast through land of its own kind and 2.6 times slower across any other, never into water; its speed found by the secant method so that the canvas is painted by a fixed moment. Meanwhile each blotch draws back off land not its own, its edge sliding along the contour lines, and melts into the paint around it; a glossy bead rides each front, and behind it the colour it brought gives way to the land’s own, laid on in strokes along the contours. The lakes are struck in last, in blue, each from its deepest point. Then the painting rises into relief and becomes the land.

  • Gravity’s spots: the Jeans instability

    Once a remnant’s gas has cooled it is unstable to its own gravity. A region larger than the Jeans length, λ_J = c √(π / Gρ), collapses; a smaller one is held up by its pressure and its turbulence (c² = c_s² + σ²/3). So the gas breaks into clumps about λ_J apart, closer where it is dense: many along the bright filaments, a few in the thin gas between, larger where the gas is hotter or more turbulent (a Poisson disc of varying radius, densest first). Each clump gathers the gas of its own cell (the points nearer it than any other, in Jeans lengths) and falls in on itself in free fall, t_ff = √(3π / 32Gρ), so the dense collapse first; a uniform sphere falls in all at once (r = r₀ cos²β, with t/t_ff = (β + sin β cos β)/(π/2)), so the gas of each cell is seen gathering toward its middle, its light squeezed brighter while the lanes between cells go dark, until it snaps into a drop of its volume with a soft bloom. First the pattern shows faintly, its contrast growing as e^{σt}. Its drop’s mass is its cell’s light times its depth, about λ_J too: the Jeans mass, ρλ_J³. The plan mirrors the shader’s gas exactly (the same hashed three-dimensional noise), so the clumps form on the filaments that are drawn. Artistic: water from a remnant’s gas.

  • Supernovae and planetary nebulae

    Science’s sky has a dozen supergiants, each coloured as a black body: Planck’s law integrated against the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions, red at 3,400 K, blue-white at 30,000 K. Moving on to Racing, seven of them end (four on a phone), the set spread farthest apart, so each remnant has room of its own and only the outer fringes of neighbours cross, in an order that is not a sweep across or down the screen. Red supergiants are the progenitors of type II-P supernovae, so the red explode as well as the blue. Each pulses faster and faster, dims as its core collapses, then breaks out in a flash that swells rather than pops, fading along the light curve of nickel-56 decaying to cobalt-56 (mean lives 8.8 and 111.3 days, compressed but in proportion), the rest of the scene dimming a little as an eye adapts; a light echo races out through the dust, and the gas near it flares in [O III], then glows in Hα as it recombines. Each remnant is a sphere seen through: it expands freely (R ∝ t), then as a Sedov–Taylor blast wave (R ∝ t^⅖), sweeping the gas round it into its shell and leaving a dark cavity. The shell is drawn by integrating along the line of sight, so it is brightest at its limb (√(1 − ρ²) − √(a² − ρ²) through each half), its gas in wisps of three-dimensional turbulence in its front half and its back half both, its inner edge rippled into Rayleigh–Taylor fingers where the ejecta push into it, knots of ejecta within lit by the reverse shock, the forward shock a thin bright rim; where two shells meet, a brighter arc. Its structure expands with it (homologous) and it cools as it grows. They differ by their physics, not their shapes: a hot progenitor’s remnant glows more in [O III], a cool one’s in Hα and [S II]. Their lines are shown as narrowband images show them, in the Hubble palette: Hα and [S II] gold and amber, [O III] teal. Artistic: the pace, compressed from millennia to a scroll.

  • A drop of paint on a canvas

    Racing’s white ice settles into a primed canvas, and thick paint lands on it a drop at a time, slowly at first and then quicker. Each drop spreads to about 0.9 We^¼ times its own radius (Clanet’s scaling, We the Weber number: how hard its inertia pushes against its surface tension), fast and then slowing as √t, overshooting a little and drawing back; its rim, decelerating, breaks into N ≈ √We / (4√3) fingers (Allen: the Rayleigh–Taylor instability of the rim), whose tips pinch off satellite drops (Rayleigh–Plateau), with a fine spray thrown farther; a drop that comes in at a slant stretches its splash and throws its spray downrange. Before it lands, its shadow closes in on it. The paint is impasto: its thickness, built up at the rim, lit as relief from the upper left (its slope from the screen’s derivatives), with a wet gloss that dries and the shadow its rim casts. Artistic: the drops’ count, sizes and colours, the canvas’s weave.

    A heightfield of the 48 four-thousand footers at their true latitudes, longitudes and heights: each a massif with concave flanks, neighbours in a range joined by ridges sagging to their cols, a highland of lesser mountains between (ridged noise), then carved by running water, hydraulic erosion by tens of thousands of droplets that pick up sediment where they run fast and steep and drop it where they slow (Beyer’s model), so ravines cut the slopes; each summit is set back to its surveyed height.   • An ABC flow

    The embers are carried by an Arnold–Beltrami–Childress flow, an exact steady solution of Euler’s equations whose streamlines are chaotic: u = (A sin z + C cos y, B sin x + A cos z, C sin y + B cos x). It has no sources or sinks, so nothing bunches or thins. Each ember’s place is found by the midpoint rule from where it left the window, and its recent path is drawn behind it as a ribbon of copper or gold. Flow time runs as 1 − e^(−P/k) of the scroll: violent at first, then all but still.

  • Gravitational capture and a dissipative disc

    The worker integrates the Paczyński–Wiita potential Φ = −GM/(r−rₛ), with Ω² = GM/[r(r−rₛ)²]; inner material orbits faster and the stability boundary is 3rₛ. Local pair friction applies equal opposite impulses along the line between particles, conserving momentum and angular momentum while removing energy into a heat and cooling ledger. This is a reduced dissipative model, not magnetohydrodynamics. Only after at least 85% of eligible shard count and mass circularize does preparation schedule the outburst. Absorbed fragments never return. Sources: Abramowicz, arXiv:0904.0913; DL-SDG, Introduction to Dissipative Particle Dynamics.

  • Schwarzschild light paths

    Precomputed rays solve u″ + u = 3u²/2 for a nonrotating black hole. The same tables bend the disc, facets, trails and faint background, including a raised secondary image. The apparent shadow radius is 3√3 rₛ/2, larger than the event horizon. Gravitational redshift and Doppler shift make the approaching side brighter. Artistic exposure keeps the black center legible. Source: Eric Bruneton, Real-time High-Quality Rendering of Non-Rotating Black Holes (2020).

  • Narrowband false colour

    Telescopes photograph a nebula one emission line at a time and give each line a colour; the Hubble palette sends sulphur, hydrogen and oxygen to red, green and blue, and balanced, its nebulae come out gold and teal. Science takes that mapping for everything: its page and experiments, its sky and liquid chrome, and the nebulae of the transition into Racing, whose drops and shards inherit their colours, down to the light Racing’s track takes in. One source (palette.js) feeds the stylesheet, the shaders and the experiments; the nebulae, lit in linear light, take its colours converted from display values.

    Science, its homepage band, and arriving at Racing

  • A flight over a heightfield

    Every pixel casts a ray from a camera that hangs over the painting while it is made, then dives to fly past Franconia Ridge, along the Garfield Ridge and over the Pemigewasset Wilderness, and turns to the Presidential Range: the ray steps through the heightfield until it meets the ground and refines the hit by bisection; a second march toward the sun gives soft shadows. The studio’s light becomes the sun as the painting rises, coming round to a low October afternoon; the light is sun, sky and bounce, the air an exponential haze integrated along each ray, thicker low down, with mist in the valleys and cloud shadows drifting. The forest is single crowns, Voronoi cells a dozen metres across (spruce and fir dark and pointed, the hardwoods rounded and each its own colour, the shadow each casts on the forest floor) in stands and groves, hemlock along the streams; above the treeline krummholz, sedge and lichen, granite with dark streaks down its slabs, October’s first snow on the highest summits. Lakes lie level, their colour by depth, rippled where the wind gusts, and mirror the mountains: a second, shorter march back up the reflected ray. The fine relief is mipmapped by each sample’s footprint, so far ground does not shimmer.

    Arriving at Outdoors, scrolled

  • Embers: fire under a crust

    The burnt glass lets go as some four thousand embers, one draw call: each a lump with a rounded, uneven outline, lit as a dome; plates of near-black char with grey ash on them, fissures wandering across where the fire shows through, and the fire itself breathing underneath, hottest in its hollows and at its rim, coloured along a black body’s ramp from deep red to white-gold. Most are small sparks (their sizes follow a power law). We close on one of them, drawn large with finer cracks and pores, and watch it cool and go out.

    Arriving at Photography, scrolled or clicked

  • A blur the vortex sharpens

    The cubes bring Science in heavily blurred, read from a mipmap pyramid of the page (a coarser level the blurrier, in five samples). The vortex’s wide base ring sharpens it: the foot of the screen first, while the vortex stands there, then, once the gathering is done, the whole page from the bottom up as the base lifts and its ring climbs, the blur shrinking continuously under the arch of its tendrils.

  • Signed distance fields

    Shapes as distance functions, so their edges stay sharp at any size: the glass card, the maze’s corridors, the crystal on the homepage.

    Racing, the homepage

  • Sphere-traced chess pieces

    The board and its Staunton pieces are drawn by sphere tracing: a ray walks the board square by square (a 2D DDA) and in each square steps through only that square’s piece, its distance the profile’s distance at (|xz|, y), with the king’s cross, the queen’s coronet, the bishop’s mitre and the rook’s crenels added as primitives. Soft shadows and ambient occlusion are marched the same way; black is obsidian (GGX highlights, a clear coat, reflections of a soft room), white is ivory (light wrapped into the surface); the lacquered board reflects them. The picture is averaged over jittered passes while nothing moves, and only the rectangle a moving piece sweeps is drawn again; a piece taken sinks into the board as into ink.

    The liquid-chrome drops are one implicit surface, the sum of r² / d² from each centre; its gradient gives the normal, so drops merge as they touch.
