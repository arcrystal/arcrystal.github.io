01 Deep space

Seven worlds around a small red star

TRAPPIST-1 is a cool red dwarf about 40 light-years away with seven Earth-sized planets, all found by the way they dim the star as they pass in front of it. Their years run from 1.5 to 19 days, locked in a chain of near-whole-number ratios, and three of them orbit where liquid water could last.

02 The geometry of plantae

Why a sunflower turns by 137.5°

Many plants set each new seed or leaf about 137.5° around from the last: the golden angle. Packed that way, seeds never line up into gaps, and the head shows interlocking spirals whose counts are neighbouring Fibonacci numbers, such as 34 and 55. Nudge the angle by a fraction of a degree and the pattern falls apart.

Move the slider away from the golden angle and watch the spirals break.

03 The extreme beauty of animalia

Colour with no pigment, flocks with no leader

A morpho butterfly’s blue is not a dye. Nanoscale ridges on its wing scales reflect light back in layers that interfere, so the colour shifts as you tilt your view. A starling murmuration has no leader either: each bird keeps track of its six or seven nearest neighbours, and thousands of those small decisions make one shape.

Move across the wing to tilt it. Move into the flock to scatter it.

04 Deep time

The mountains that made life

Twice in Earth’s history, as supercontinents came together, mountain ranges as high as the Himalayas and up to 8,000 km long rose across them. They are known only from their roots: tiny zircon crystals, low in lutetium, that form under the pressure deep beneath high mountains. As those supermountains wore away they washed phosphorus and iron into the oceans. The first, the Nuna Supermountain, 2.0 to 1.8 billion years ago, coincides with the likely appearance of eukaryotes, the complex cells that plants and animals are made of. The second, the Transgondwanan, 650 to 500 million years ago, coincides with the first large animals and, about 45 million years later, the Cambrian explosion. In between, with no supermountains, came the “Boring Billion”, when life barely changed. It is a correlation the researchers propose to test, not a proven cause.

Move across the timeline to stop it at any moment; the gold and blue grains are the nutrients reaching the sea.

05 Human discovery and mystery

Still listening

Voyager 1 left Earth in 1977, crossed into interstellar space in 2012, and is the farthest thing people have ever made, still sending word home across a gap light takes most of a day to cross. The Drake equation, written in 1961, is how we argue about who else might be out there to hear: seven factors, most of them still guesses.

06 Computer science

A model cast in silicon

A GPU runs a language model as software: its billions of weights stream in from memory for every word it writes, and memory, not arithmetic, sets the pace. Taalas turned that around. Its first chip, the HC1, builds one model, Llama 3.1 8B, into the silicon itself, its weights stored on the die beside the circuits that use them, in a 6 nm chip of 53 billion transistors. The company reports about 17,000 tokens a second for a single user, against about 230 on an Nvidia H200 (independent tests measured 14,000 to 16,000), and says a new model can be cast into a chip in about two months. The cost of all that speed: the HC1 can run that one model and nothing else.

Both lanes start together, in real time. The chip has finished before the GPU has written its first sentence.
